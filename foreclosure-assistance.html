What Happens When a Mortgage Becomes Delinquent?

A mortgage becomes delinquent when a scheduled payment is not made by its due date. If the missed payment is not brought current, the account can fall further behind, and each additional missed payment increases the total amount owed.

Once an account is delinquent, servicers commonly begin to contact the homeowner, and fees or other charges may be added to the account in line with the loan documents. The delinquency is typically reported to the credit reporting agencies, which can affect the homeowner's credit history.

In many cases, a situation is easier to resolve the earlier it is addressed, because more options may still be open and fewer deadlines will have passed. Waiting while correspondence goes unanswered tends to narrow the choices available.

What Is the Foreclosure Process?

Foreclosure is a legal process, governed by the laws of the state where the property is located and by the terms of the mortgage documents. Because it is governed by state law, the process differs significantly from one state to another.

Although the details vary, the stages often include a formal notice of default or breach, a reinstatement or cure period during which the homeowner may be able to bring the loan current, and, if the matter is not resolved, a scheduled sale of the property. Some states use a judicial process, in which the lender must go through the courts, while others use a non-judicial process that follows a different set of steps and timelines. The terminology used at each stage also differs by state.

Because timelines, notices and terminology vary so much, it is essential that homeowners find out exactly what applies in their own state and seek advice that is specific to their situation.

What Is Loss Mitigation?

Loss mitigation is the servicer's process for reviewing alternatives to foreclosure. The aim is to evaluate whether an option other than foreclosure is possible, based on the homeowner's circumstances and the servicer's and investor's guidelines.

Loss mitigation commonly includes options such as a loan modification, a repayment plan, a period of forbearance, a short sale, or a deed in lieu of foreclosure where those options are available. Which options exist, and whether a homeowner might qualify, depends on the servicer, the investor, the loan type and the homeowner's financial position.

Reviewing these options usually requires a complete application package, including financial documentation. A partial or incomplete submission can slow the process, so it is worth confirming exactly what the servicer requires.

Can a Mortgage Modification Be Considered During Foreclosure?

In some circumstances a mortgage modification can be considered while a foreclosure is under way. Whether it is reviewed depends on the servicer, the investor, the type of loan and how far the foreclosure process has progressed.

Timelines can be tight once foreclosure has started, and the window in which an application can be submitted and reviewed may be limited. Homeowners should not assume that they will be reviewed automatically; it may be necessary to apply for the option and to follow up with the servicer.

Applying for a modification does not, by itself, stop or pause a foreclosure, and it should never be treated as a way to protect legal rights. If a foreclosure is under way, independent legal advice is important in addition to any loss mitigation application.

What Should You Do If You Receive a Foreclosure Notice?

Read the notice carefully and note every deadline it contains, including the dates by which any amount must be paid or any action must be taken. Keep the original in a safe place.

Contact your mortgage servicer promptly and ask which loss mitigation options may be available to you and exactly what documents they require. Keep copies of everything you send and everything you receive.

Keep a written log of your calls, recording the date, the name of the person you spoke with and what was discussed. This record can be valuable if questions arise later. Alongside this, it is strongly advisable to seek independent legal advice from a qualified foreclosure attorney in your state.

What If a Foreclosure Sale Has Already Been Scheduled?

If a sale has already been scheduled, it is important to act immediately rather than to wait. Contact your mortgage servicer without delay and ask what options or requirements apply at this stage.

It is also important to consult a qualified foreclosure attorney in the state where the property is located. An attorney can explain any requirements that may apply to postponing or cancelling a scheduled sale under the law of that state.

Do not rely on an online application, or on this website, to protect your legal rights or to stop a foreclosure. Only the steps required by your servicer, the loan documents and the law of your state can do that, and only your servicer and, where relevant, the courts can change the status of a scheduled sale.

Why Is It Important to Respond to Your Mortgage Servicer?

Servicers generally must attempt to make contact with a borrower whose mortgage is delinquent. Responding to that contact keeps the homeowner informed about the status of the account and about the options that may be available.

Staying in contact also preserves access to the loss mitigation review process. When correspondence is ignored, opportunities to submit an application, provide documents or meet a deadline can be missed.

Ignoring letters or calls reduces the options that remain available and can make an already difficult situation harder to resolve. Even where a homeowner is unsure what to do, making contact is usually a better starting point than waiting.

Where to Get Independent Help

Homeowners do not have to face these decisions alone. A HUD-approved housing counselling agency can provide free assistance and is a good place to start for independent, homeowner-focused guidance. Homeowners can also seek advice from a qualified attorney, who can explain their rights and options under the law of their own state.

These sources of help are independent of MMCV. MMCV is not affiliated with HUD or with any government body, and this page does not describe or endorse the requirements of any specific program.

Important Compliance Statement

MMCV cannot stop, delay or prevent a foreclosure, and applying through MMCV does not automatically pause foreclosure proceedings. Any decision about a mortgage account, a loss mitigation option or a foreclosure rests with the applicable mortgage servicer and/or investor, and in some matters with the courts, and not with MMCV. Homeowners should always seek independent legal advice where foreclosure is a possibility.
