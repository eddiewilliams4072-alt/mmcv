Understanding Mortgage Modification

Learn how mortgage modifications work, what homeowners may need to qualify, and what to expect during the process.

A mortgage modification changes one or more terms of an existing mortgage loan. What Is a Mortgage Modification?

A mortgage modification is a permanent change to one or more terms of an existing mortgage loan. The change is agreed between the homeowner and the party that owns or manages the loan, and it is designed to make the mortgage more manageable in the homeowner's current circumstances.

Depending on the servicer's guidelines, a modification may change the interest rate, extend the length of the loan, move past-due amounts into the loan balance, or adjust how the loan is repaid. The original loan is not replaced by a new loan, and the agreement remains subject to the servicer's and the investor's requirements.

A modification is not a loan provided by MMCV. Only the mortgage servicer, working within its own and its investor's guidelines, can decide whether a modification is offered and on what terms. Approval is never guaranteed.

How Does a Mortgage Modification Work?

The process usually begins with the homeowner gathering information about their mortgage and their household finances. This information is then submitted to the servicer, often as a completed application together with supporting documents.

The servicer reviews the submission under its own guidelines and those of the investor that owns the loan. During the review, the servicer may ask for further documents or clarification, and it may take some time to reach a decision.

When the review is complete, the servicer communicates a decision. If the request is approved, the homeowner generally receives a written agreement that must be signed and returned by a stated deadline. In some cases the servicer also asks the homeowner to complete a trial period, such as a number of reduced payments, before the modification becomes permanent. Failing to meet the conditions of the agreement may mean the offer no longer applies.

Who May Qualify?

Each servicer and investor applies its own eligibility criteria, so there is no single set of rules that applies to every homeowner. In general terms, servicers commonly consider the reason for the financial hardship, whether the hardship is likely to be temporary or permanent, the homeowner's current income and household expenses, the amount that is past due, and the type of loan involved.

Because these factors are specific to each household and each loan, this page cannot tell you whether you will qualify. MMCV cannot determine eligibility, approve a modification or predict an outcome.

Only the mortgage servicer and, where applicable, the investor can decide whether a homeowner qualifies and what terms, if any, will be offered.

What Documents Are Commonly Required?

Servicers frequently request financial documentation to assess a homeowner's circumstances. Although the exact list varies, the following items are commonly requested:

  • Your most recent mortgage statement.
  • Recent pay statements showing your income.
  • Proof of other income, such as benefits or self-employment income.
  • Recent bank statements, when the servicer requests them.
  • A written hardship letter explaining your situation.
  • Information about your household expenses.
  • Any other documents the servicer specifically requests.

Requirements vary between servicers and can change over time, so it is important to confirm the current list with your servicer. Submitting a complete and consistent package is usually the most straightforward way to avoid delays caused by missing information.

How Can a Modification Affect Monthly Payments?

A modification can change several aspects of a loan at once. It may adjust the interest rate, extend the remaining term of the loan, move past-due amounts into the loan balance, or change the way the unpaid principal is treated. Any combination of these changes may be included in a single agreement.

Because these adjustments interact with one another, the effect on the monthly payment can vary. A modification may lower the monthly amount, leave it broadly unchanged, or in some cases increase it, for example where past-due amounts are added to the balance or the term is shortened.

A reduction in the monthly payment is never guaranteed. The amount depends on the specific terms the servicer and investor are prepared to offer, and on the homeowner's circumstances.

What Happens After an Application Is Submitted?

Once a complete package has been received, the servicer usually acknowledges the submission and begins its review. The length of this review period varies considerably between servicers and can depend on the completeness of the information provided.

It is common for the servicer to request additional documents or clarification while the review is under way. Responding promptly to these requests can help keep the review moving.

The servicer then communicates a decision. If the modification is approved, the homeowner can usually expect a written agreement setting out the new terms, along with any conditions such as a trial payment period, a date by which the agreement must be signed and returned, and any other steps required to complete the process.

What Happens If an Application Is Denied?

If a modification is not approved, it can help to ask the servicer, in writing where possible, for the reason for the decision. Understanding the reason may indicate whether any information was missing or whether a different option might be more suitable.

It is also worth asking the servicer which alternative loss mitigation options may still be available, since a denial of one option does not necessarily mean that no option remains.

Homeowners may wish to seek independent advice from a HUD-approved housing counselling agency or a qualified attorney about their own situation. A denial does not by itself prevent a homeowner from contacting their servicer about other options for the loan.

Other Mortgage Assistance Options

Alongside modification, servicers may offer other forms of loss mitigation. These can include a repayment plan to clear past-due amounts over time, a period of forbearance during a temporary hardship, a partial claim or similar arrangement where it is available, or reinstatement by paying the amount owed.

Each option has different effects on the loan and different requirements, and availability depends on the servicer, the investor, the loan type and the homeowner's circumstances. None of these options should be assumed to be available, and the servicer retains the final decision in every case.

Understanding the range of options can help homeowners ask better questions of their servicer and make more informed decisions.
